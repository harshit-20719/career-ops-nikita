03

What to send

Form Q3 · Why Unbox Health, and why now? (3–5 lines, informal)

I spent four years at Tata Trusts deciding which health technologies deserved funding, which mostly meant separating what genuinely worked from what had worked once in favourable conditions. Unbox is the first consumer company I have seen where that judgement is the product rather than a function inside it.

Why now: I left the Trusts in September, and I would rather spend the next few years on the side of the market that makes claims checkable than on the side that funds them.

Form Q6 · Something you’ve built

I co-founded Tiff.in, an edutainment business making long and short-form content on Indian history for 16 to 35 year olds. It was one of three teams funded out of 35 at Ashoka University’s incubator.

The problem was that history content in India was either academic or inaccurate, and nobody under 35 was watching either. I built the market sizing, the competitive landscape and the unit economics, turned them into investor-ready projections, and pitched to 20+ funds and to the founders of a major Indian e-commerce platform. On the demand side I ran content and advertising across four platforms.

What changed: 520% growth in YouTube views and a 370% increase in Instagram followers. What I learned is the part relevant to you — the audience grew when we stopped arguing that the content was good and started making it easy to check that it was accurate.

Form Q7 · 7 lakh to 7 crore users a month (5 lines or fewer)

A rating only changes a purchase if it is present when the purchase happens, so the answer is distribution rather than audience: the grade has to sit on the quick-commerce and marketplace listing, not on unboxhealth.in.

Second, concentrate rather than broaden. 70 categories at 7 lakh users is thin; protein, supplements and staples are where people already distrust labels, and depth there is what makes the brand mean something.

Third, the comparison page is the shareable unit, not the rating — “A vs B” is what gets sent in a family group, and it should be built to be sent.

Fourth, a D grade is news. Each one is a story a journalist will run, and you already have the lab report attached.

Fifth, 100x on a trust product fails the moment one grade is shown to be wrong, so the re-test cadence has to scale with the audience.

Form Q8 · One thing done well, one thing missing

Done well: the letter grade with the full lab report and method published behind it. A grade is legible in one second and auditable in ten minutes, and almost nothing else in Indian consumer health gives you both. Keeping brands out of the rating is what makes the grade worth anything, and it is the harder commercial choice.

Missing, or at least not visible to me: how the independence survives the business model. “Get your products rated” sits a scroll away from “zero brand influence, ads or paid placements”, and if brands ever pay to be tested, a reader has to be told exactly what the money buys. I would publish the fee, state that the grade publishes whichever way the result falls, and show one case where a paying brand scored a D.

And a second, smaller one: the “previously rated” products imply grades go stale when formulations change, but nothing tells me how old a grade is or when it will be checked again. A visible test date and re-test policy is the cheapest trust upgrade on the site. I spent four years setting evidence bars and defending them to a board, and the question I always got was not “is this true” but “is this still true”.

04

They will ask

“Your health and nutrition habits” — the form question, not an interview one

Write this one yourself. It asks for one habit you recently adopted and why, and one you have not managed to adopt and what is in the way. It is checking whether you actually care about the category, and an invented answer will not survive the follow-up conversation. If you genuinely have nothing to put here, that is useful information: it means they are looking for a consumer-health obsessive and you would be the wrong hire. Two honest lines beat anything polished.

"You come from healthcare funding, not consumer. Why would you be good at distribution or brand?"

Answer it as a transfer rather than a claim: you have never run a brand, and the thing you do have is four years of deciding what evidence is strong enough to act on, in a company whose product is exactly that. Then point at Tiff.in for the consumer half — four platforms, 520% and 370%, and the unit economics behind it.

“Are you based in Bangalore?”

The form asks this outright and the honest answer is no. They say in-person matters. Have the relocation answer ready and volunteer it rather than waiting: you are in Mumbai, Bengaluru is a city you are actively targeting, and you can be there. Saying “no” with no follow-up is what kills this application.

"How would you handle a brand threatening to sue over a D grade?"

A real risk for this business and nobody will have thought about it. Your answer is procedural, which is the right register: the grade rests on the published method and the lab report, so the defence is built before the grade goes out. You have made the equivalent call — setting a heavier evidence bar and holding it when a partner pushed back.

"What would you own in the first three months?"

The posting invites you to define your own problem, so arrive with one. The strongest is the one in your form answer: the trust architecture around the ratings — test dates, re-test cadence, and how brand-paid testing is disclosed. It is unglamorous, it is squarely your discipline, and it is the thing that breaks first if they 100x.

05

Ask them

  1. How is testing funded today — do brands pay to be rated, and if so what does that money buy and not buy?
  2. What is the re-test policy when a formulation changes? How do you know a grade is still true?
  3. Where does revenue come from now, and where is it meant to come from in two years?
  4. 7 lakh users a month — how many come back, and what do they do on the second visit?
  5. Which category actually drives the traffic, and which one do you wish did?
  6. What does hybrid mean in practice, and how many days in the Bengaluru office?
  7. This is an open application, so what are the two or three seats you are actually trying to fill?

Hold two back for a later round — they will ask again.

06

Know before the interview

  • “Health, wellness and nutrition aren’t just a category for you — they’re personal.” They mean it, and the form asks about your own habits twice over. This is the one application on your board that screens for enthusiasm rather than experience, and it is the honest reason to skip it if the enthusiasm is not there.
  • You are not in Bangalore and the form asks. They say in-person matters and they are hybrid. This is the single biggest drag on odds and the reason ODDS sits at 3.2 rather than 3.6.
  • The expected-compensation field is required and open-ended. No band is published anywhere, you have no comp floor in this search, and an unconsidered number is the easiest way to be screened out or to anchor yourself low. Decide it deliberately.
  • Consumer and FMCG register throughout. This would be the eighth role of the search where the healthcare differentiator is partly spent, though less so than FirstClub or Fireside since the subject matter is at least health.
  • No funding or team detail is public beyond press coverage, and the revenue model is not stated anywhere on the site. Ask about it rather than assuming there is one.
  • 31 applicants is the real reason to do this. Every other role in this week’s scan had 200+. A low-competition application with no credential gate and a human reading the answers is a better use of an hour than a tailored CV into a 200-person queue.
