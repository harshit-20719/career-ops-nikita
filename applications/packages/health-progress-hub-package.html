What to send

Q: The project you are most proud of, and one thing you would do differently now · 120 words

Use the geospatial heat map. It is your strongest piece of work and it is the closest thing on your CV to what HPH actually sells.

Facts to use: every rupee the Trusts deployed, mapped across Indian states, scored and compared by geography and sub-theme; it showed identical interventions producing materially different results by region and terrain; it directly shaped portfolio allocation from 2022 to 2024, changing where capital went and which programmes were concentrated on.

The “differently now” half is the half they are actually reading. A weak answer makes it cosmetic. Pick something real and structural: the model scored returns but did not separate how much of the variation came from the intervention versus the delivery conditions around it, so it told you where results differed without fully explaining why. That is an honest limitation of a weighted-factor approach and it is exactly the methodological conversation they want to have.

Checkable by: this one is internal. Name a partner-organisation contact from a programme whose allocation the map changed, or say plainly that the output is internal to the Trusts and offer to walk through the method on a call.

Q: A period with three or more pieces of work at once, how you tracked them, and one moment tracking caught a problem first · 100 words

The dual mandate is the answer. You hold both Program Officer and Grants Manager responsibilities, the only person on the team carrying two roles, across 40+ programmes.

The tracking facts: portfolio dashboards you built and presented to the CEO; fund utilisation tracking and variance analysis across the whole portfolio; screening 30 to 50 proposals a month against a fixed framework while design cycles of two to three months ran in parallel.

The “caught it first” moment is the part that needs a specific incident, and only you have it. The natural place to look is your gatekeeping role: you evaluated every partner organisation’s budget in the health theme for gaps, and for whether the numbers actually reflected programme activity and intensity, before anything reached CEO and Board approval. There will be a case where variance analysis surfaced a problem before the partner reported it. Use that one, with the detail.

Q: A time you knew in advance your work would be late, and told the person waiting · 100 words

I cannot draft this one for you and you should be wary of anyone who offers to. It needs a real incident: what you said, how far ahead, and what happened.

What they are testing is named explicitly in the job description: “If a project becomes unmanageable or proves more difficult than you envisioned, you don’t shy away from re-scoping it or asking for help.” So the answer they want is not heroic recovery. It is early, specific escalation and a re-scope.

Where to look in your own record: a design cycle that ran past two to three months because a partner dependency slipped, or a Board-bound recommendation that needed more diligence than the cycle allowed. The strong version names the person, the lead time, and the decision that followed.

Do not pick an example where it turned out fine because you worked a weekend. That answers a different question and a team that publishes its pay rates will read it as the wrong instinct.

Q: Building context on an unfamiliar domain in days, then making a judgement someone acted on · 80 words

Two candidates, both real. Pick whichever you remember in more detail.

The stronger one, if you have the specifics: your technology pilots. You structured them under a deliberately heavier evidence bar, evaluating the technology across varied conditions rather than favourable ones, and selecting geographies that limited community risk, externalities and confounding factors. Health-tech and AI diagnostics were owned sub-themes, so you were forming views on which technologies were ready to deploy and which worked once, in one place, with the founder present. Name a specific technology, the window you had, and whether the judgement held.

The alternative: your original remit covered six sub-themes — education, WASH, nutrition, health, rural upliftment, urban poverty — scored on a 40-parameter framework, with course corrections proposed and Board advice given on which investments were working. Moving between six domains at speed is the same muscle.

You have not managed researchers. Q: Research or analysis in global health, development or health policy that you did yourself · 60 words

This is the one question where you have a public, linkable artifact. Use it.

“Persistence of Informal Social Networks in the Indian Textile Market: A Case Study of Surat”, Journal of Enterprising Culture, September 2023, Volume 31 Issue 3. You built a comparative metric for the role of social networks in formal versus informal business from interviews across the entire supply chain, from weavers and manufacturers to end consumers, and applied the World Bank’s Doing Business methodology to gauge informality and network dependence.

It is not global health, so say so rather than stretching it. Then add the heat map and the in-progress US-versus-India health economies paper as the health-specific work, if the word count allows.

Q: Prioritisation research — say YES. Then: strongest line, weakest line, and the country question

Yes. This is your lane, it is their largest area of work, they say more organisations ask for it than they can serve, and the AIM cohort arrives from late November.

Weakest line, 40 words. Do not manufacture modesty, and do not pick something that disqualifies you. Say how you are closing it, concretely, not aspirationally.

And the one that matters most: “Pick an intervention and a country you know well. You can answer this better than almost anyone who applies, and it is the single strongest thing in your whole application. You have the finding, from your own data: identical interventions produced materially different results across Indian regions and terrains. And you have the design response: you built government linkages specifically to avoid creating parallel infrastructure, which is the most common and most expensive thing a foreign charity gets wrong in India. Pick one intervention, name the state, and say what breaks. Concrete beats general here by a wide margin.

Q: Research strategy — also YES, and the evidence-gap question

Say yes. It only starts from December, so it sequences after the sprint rather than competing with it, and it is where the build-from-zero value sits: “develop HPH’s research agenda… the questions worth answering, who else is working on them.”

If you are short on time, this is the section to answer adequately and prioritisation is the section to answer excellently. Do not let a thin research-strategy answer dilute a strong prioritisation one.

The evidence gap, 80 words. You ran into a real one and it came out of your own work: identical health interventions produced materially different returns across Indian geographies, and nobody has systematically explained why. Answer their three parts — where you hit it (building the heat map, when the pattern showed up and the model could not account for it), why it would unlock other work (every funder and charity choosing where to deploy is guessing at the same question), and why nobody has answered it (the data sits inside individual funders’ portfolios, is never pooled, and no single organisation has both the data and a reason to publish it).

That last clause is the strong part. “Why has nobody answered it” is where most applicants will be vague, and you have a structural reason rather than a shrug.

“Name one kind of task we should not assign you” — required, and I am not inventing this one. It asks what you do not enjoy or are not good at, and a fabricated answer here is both dishonest and easy to catch in a live work test. Tell me what it actually is and I will help you phrase it. What it should not be: anything central to the role, and not a humblebrag. What it could honestly be, if true: work with no decision attached to it at the end.

Optional “one thing we could help you with”: worth using, not skipping. The honest ask is methodological — formal training in the quantitative side of prioritisation modelling, which is the gap you named as your weakest line. Asking for the thing you already admitted is consistent, and they offered.

Where did you hear about this opportunity: answer plainly and accurately.

Q: AI disclosure — the exact shape of an honest answer

Disclose. Undisclosed use found later disqualifies the application, and they say they run detection tools.

The honest description of what happened here: you used an AI assistant to assemble the questions in advance, to identify which of your own experiences answered each one, and to check wording and word counts. The examples, the judgements and the sentences are yours.

They will ask

“What is a foreign charity likely to get wrong in India?”

They tell you in the form that they will come back to this on the call, so treat the written answer as an opening and prepare the long version. Have one intervention, one state, and a specific failure mode, plus what you would have done instead. Your parallel-infrastructure point is the spine: programmes that build their own delivery layer alongside a government system that already exists, then cannot hand over.

“Walk us through the heat map.”

Near-certain, because it is the most relevant thing you have and you will have cited it three or four times across the form. Be ready on method, not just result: what the parameters were, how they were weighted, what the data source was, where the model was weakest. A team whose whole product is prioritisation methodology will probe the method.

"Why a part-time contract, when your CV is a full-time portfolio role?"

They will ask, and the honest answer is good: you want to do this specific work, you can commit 12 real hours, and you would rather be accurate about capacity than promise 40 and deliver 15. Their own words are “we appreciate honesty over more availability.” Do not improvise around your current situation — decide the sentence in advance and keep it short.

"You have not managed researchers. Why should we put you on research management?"

Fair question, since it is the one mandatory stream. Answer with the adjacent muscle rather than a denial: you gave direct feedback to founders and programme leaders whose domains you did not hold, on work your recommendation then depended on. Then say what you would need from them in week one, which signals you have thought about the gap rather than hoping they miss it.

“What would you do with the AIM cohort choosing a first country?”

This is their live engagement from late November, so it is the most likely scenario question. Have a method ready: what you would want to know before scoring anything, which parameters you would refuse to weight without local validation, and how you would test the model against one country someone on the ground knows well. That last step is their stated view of what goes wrong, so showing it unprompted lands.

  2. You are three people at about 1.5 FTE and you have just received funding. How many hires does this round cover, and where does this role sit in that?

  4. The sprint runs to 15 November with mentors across African governments and US-based founders. What does the meeting rhythm actually look like, and what is expected synchronously?
  5. You publish your pay and you review in 48 hours. What else do you do differently from how most non-profits run, and what has that cost you?
  6. If this goes well in six months, what does the extended version look like — more hours, a different scope, or a different title?

Hold two back for a later round — they will ask again.

Know before the interview

  • FIT is 1 as a job, and that is not a formality. Sector gate fails; both carve-out doors close (AI is one of four research areas rather than the substance, the title is ‘Officer’, and there is no principal whose orbit is itself a credential). Full-time this is a lateral back into the sector you are leaving. Part-time it is a tool. Nothing about the kit changes that line.
  • What it builds is analytical modelling, not financial modelling. A weighted factor model is not a DCF, a comparables set or a cap table. This strengthens an answer to “strong analytical and quantitative modelling” and gives you a second, current, non-Tata reference for it. It does not answer 2070 Health’s “proficiency in valuation” or Artha’s buy-side modelling bar. Those still need something else, and claiming otherwise would repeat the Tiff.in error.
  • Verifiability is the structural problem with your application. Your best material is internal to the Trusts, and they state plainly that unverifiable answers carry less weight. The partner-organisation referee route is the fix, and it needs doing before you write.
  • Employment contract risk. Paid contract work for another organisation while employed full-time may breach an exclusivity clause. Check before accepting, not before applying — applying costs you nothing either way.
  • The process is about six hours — form, 2.5-hour paid test task, two interviews, a live work test — for a part-time six-month contract. The test task being paid offsets some of that. Worth going in knowing the price.
  • “Remote, any time zone” is a statement about where you sit, not about when the calls are. Ask the meeting-rhythm question. Your timezone constraint has been absolute since day one and this is the only gate still genuinely open.
  • On a CV, ‘Research & Scaling Officer’ sitting under ‘Program Officer’ reads as more of the same to a commercial reader. List it as a contract engagement, with the hours, rather than as your new role.
  • The team is three people at roughly 1.5 FTE, newly funded, and this is their first hire. That is genuine upside on scope and genuine fragility on stability. The six-month contract is honest about which.
